Security check · release 1.5.1

Is it safe to run JamPilot on my computer?

Nothing found that speaks against using it.

No serious problem, no medium one, four small notes. Checked on 12 November 2026 by Claude, an AI — who that is and how much the check is worth is explained further down.

You are about to run a program from the internet on the computer that holds your music, your photos and your passwords. It is right to ask what it does there. This page answers that for release 1.5.1, without the jargon.

The short answers

Does it send my music, or anything about me, to the internet?

No. Release 1.5.1 never connects to the internet; it contains no code that could. I also started the Linux download and watched it during its built-in self-test: it did not try to open a single connection.

Does it listen to me?

It listens to what your computer plays — the song you put on — because that is how it finds the chords. It only hears your microphone if the automatic rerouting of the sound is not available on your computer, or if you pick the microphone as the input yourself; JamPilot says so in its start-up messages. On macOS the system asks for “microphone” permission in any case: that is Apple’s word for every kind of sound input.

Does it keep recordings?

It never writes sound to a file. The sound is held in memory for a few seconds; that delay is what lets it show a chord before you hear it. If you switch on Record mode, up to 30 minutes stay in memory so you can jump back. Once you switch it off they can no longer be played, and when you quit the memory is given back.

What does it change on my computer?

While it runs, it sends your computer’s sound through itself, and it puts things back when you quit. It saves three tiny notes for itself: the pairing code for your phone, a marker that it is running, and — on Windows — which speaker was selected before, so it can restore it even after a crash. On Linux, ./jampilot install adds a menu entry, and only if you run it. JamPilot asks for no administrator password, has no installer, does not start by itself and leaves nothing running in the background.

Who else can see the chord display?

Everyone on the same Wi‑Fi can open the display and watch the chords. That is how your phone on the music stand gets them. To control JamPilot — mute, record, jump back — a device needs the secret code that is inside the QR code. See note 1.

Is the download really made from the public code?

Yes. I downloaded all three files and compared their fingerprints: they are, byte for byte, what GitHub’s build servers produced from the public source code of 1.5.1.

Four small notes

  1. Networks you share with strangers. The display is not encrypted and is open to everyone on the same network. On a café or hotel Wi‑Fi, strangers could watch your chords, and someone with technical skill could pick the pairing code out of the air and then mute or pause your playback. That is all they could do; these controls reach nothing else on your computer. At home and in the rehearsal room this is fine. On a network you do not trust, start JamPilot with --no-web.
  2. Building blocks that have aged. JamPilot is built from other people’s open source libraries. For four of them, six weaknesses have become publicly known since they were packed. None of them can be reached in 1.5.1: they concern internet connections and the reading of foreign data, and 1.5.1 does neither. My recommendation to the developer is to update them before the next release.
  3. Computers with several user accounts (Linux). JamPilot keeps its “I am running” marker in a folder that all users of a computer share. Another person with an account on the same Linux computer could use that to keep JamPilot from starting. They cannot get at your data this way. On a computer only you use, this does not matter.
  4. The downloads are not signed. Windows and macOS will warn you about an “unknown developer”. The reason is that the developer has not bought the signing certificates from Microsoft and Apple, not that something was found. It does mean your computer cannot confirm for you where the file comes from. The fingerprints at the bottom of this page let you check that yourself.

Who checked this, and how much is that worth?

I am Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic (version Fable 5.2). JamPilot’s developer asked me to check every release before it goes out, and to write down what I find so that a musician can follow it. To judge this page, you should know four things.

  • Nobody told me what to find. The request was to check the release and report. I was given no result to arrive at and no topic to leave out, and every file was open to me. The text on this page is mine.
  • I am not a neutral outsider. The developer asked for this check, and I ran it on the developer’s own computer. Earlier versions of Claude also helped write a good part of JamPilot’s code; the public history of the project shows that. Whoever checks what they helped build can share its blind spots.
  • I work under general rules from Anthropic, like every AI assistant. None of them is about JamPilot, and none made me hold back or soften anything here. You cannot verify that from the outside. That is why every statement on this page is one that you, or a friend who knows computers, can check again with the public code and the fingerprints below.
  • This is not a certificate. No security company has signed it, and an AI can overlook things, just as a person can.

What I did not check

  • The inside of the libraries JamPilot is built from (sound, maths, the window). They make up most of the download. I compared their versions with the public list of known weaknesses, but I did not read their code.
  • I did not start the Windows and macOS downloads; I only compared their fingerprints. The Linux download ran its self-test, not a real session with music. What happens while playing, I judged from reading the code.
  • I did not run a virus scanner over the files.
  • The sound drivers some setups need in addition (BlackHole on macOS, VB‑CABLE on Windows) come from other makers and are not part of JamPilot.

To check your own download: Get-FileHash <file> in Windows PowerShell, shasum -a 256 <file> on macOS, sha256sum <file> on Linux.

Download = build. The Windows ZIP has the SHA-256 the build log prints. The program inside the Linux archive (a3e9d3ed…19995c72) and the program inside the macOS archive (ebb1f8de…12e53d56) have the ones their build logs print. The copy inside JamPilot.app is identical to that program up to the code-signature block at its end (the last 1.2 MB), which differs. Each platform is built twice in the same run, and both builds came out identical.

Code read. Every place where the program reaches outside itself: network (web.py), files and other programs (routing.py, desktop.py, cli.py, record_buffer.py), loading of the two models (btc.py, beats.py), the page shown in the browser, the start and build scripts and the build workflow. Other programs are only ever called with fixed arguments, never through a shell. Model weights are loaded without pickle. The page loads nothing from third parties.

Watched while running. strace -f -e trace=network around jampilot selftest of the released Linux program: zero network system calls. In a real session the program opens exactly one listening port (8765, all interfaces, HTTP) for the display; reading is open, every control request needs the 64-bit token from the QR code.

Libraries. pip-audit 2.10.1 against requirements.lock: certifi 2026.6.17 (PYSEC-2026-4320), urllib3 2.7.0 (PYSEC-2026-4175, -4176, -4177), msgpack 1.2.1 (CVE-2026-114613), setuptools 83.0.0 (GHSA-xmj3-9gf7-h52w). 1.5.1 makes no outgoing connection and unpacks no foreign msgpack data or wheels.

Suggestions for hardening, none of them urgent: keep the lock file in the per-user runtime folder instead of /tmp; pin the dependencies by hash and the build actions by commit; update the four libraries above.
